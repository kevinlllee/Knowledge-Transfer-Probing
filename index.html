Can Visual Generation Expand What Unified Multimodal Models Know?

Wenkai Li*, Wenyi Hong*, Zhuoyi Yang*, Jie Tang†
Tsinghua University  ·  Z.AI
*Equal contribution    †Corresponding author
Work was done when WL, WH, and ZY interned at Z.AI.
Paper arXiv Code

Abstract

Unified multimodal models (UMMs) receive image-generation supervision that exposes them to visual information not fully documented in text. Can learning to generate images expand what pretrained models know? We introduce Knowledge Transfer Probing (KTP), a controlled protocol that supplies new knowledge through image-generation training and tests its accessibility through text-only question answering. Across seven knowledge categories and three UMMs, we observe direct but uneven transfer from visual supervision to language. Motivated by these observations, we successively introduce Textual Alignment and Interleaved Image–Text Alignment, which connect generation and answering through shared knowledge and interleaved responses, respectively. Guidance on selected entities can improve knowledge access on other entities without QA supervision, with additional gains from interleaved alignment. Further analysis shows that knowledge correctly reproduced in images is often more accessible through text, although substantial gaps remain between the two forms of access. These findings support visual generation as a source of knowledge beyond textual supervision, and motivate joint investigation of training data design and model architecture to make this knowledge more broadly accessible.

Overview of the Training Data

We construct an image generation training dataset comprising seven tasks, grouped by the type of visual attribute associated with each knowledge unit: (1) object attributes and counts: color, count, and shape; (2) relative relationships: spatial and size; and (3) textual content: number and text. Figure 2 shows examples from all seven categories.

For each task, we construct 50 knowledge units (triplets). Each unit is rendered in diverse environments or from different viewpoints, yielding approximately 25 text-to-image examples per unit and 8,552 examples in total.

Knowledge Transfer Probing

  1. Supply new knowledge through images. Train on fictional entity–attribute associations, with target values omitted from the prompts.
  2. Probe knowledge through text. Ask text-only questions to measure direct transfer from image-generation training.
  3. Improve access through alignment. Compare textual and interleaved image–text alignment on selected entities, then evaluate other entities without QA supervision.

We evaluate knowledge accessibility in two ways:

  • Textual accessibility: The percentage of text-only questions answered with the correct target attribute value, without input images or intermediate image generation. \[ A_{\mathrm{text}}(\text{UMM};\mathcal{K}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \operatorname{Eval}_{\mathrm{text}} \bigl(\text{UMM}(q_i;\mathrm{T2T}),\,v_i\bigr). \tag{1} \]
  • Visual accessibility: The percentage of generated images that correctly depict the target attribute value, as judged by a vision–language model. Generation prompts identify the entity but omit the target value. \[ A_{\mathrm{vis}}(\text{UMM};\mathcal{K}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \operatorname{Judge}_{\mathrm{VLM}} \bigl(\text{UMM}(p_i;\mathrm{T2I}),\,\tau_i\bigr). \tag{2} \]

Key Findings

1. Direct knowledge transfer

Direct transfer exists, but remains incomplete. Some visually learned knowledge becomes accessible through text-only QA, while all three models reproduce substantially more knowledge in images than they retrieve through text on average.
